\begin{abstract}\noindent
Housing costs are at the center of the public debate on declining fertility.
Children need space, and young households, who have little savings, find
family space costly to pay for. In this paper, I study how the cost of family
space shapes fertility and what housing policy can do about it. I build a
life-cycle model in which households choose whether to have children,
whether to rent or own, how much space to occupy and how much to save, under
uninsurable earnings risk and a down-payment constraint. House prices and
rents are determined in equilibrium, and births today determine the number of
households that compete for housing a generation later. I calibrate the model
to the United States in 2007 and study the transition that follows the
decline in fertility. Easier access to mortgage credit mostly changes the
timing of births. Policies that lower the cost of family space in every
period, such as expanding the housing supply, or that make the commitment a
child entails safer, such as reducing earnings risk, raise completed
fertility.
\end{abstract}
