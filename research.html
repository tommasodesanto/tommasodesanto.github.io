Research

  • Job Market Paper Fertility and Housing Tenure Choice — Paper (coming soon)
    Abstract

    I study how access to housing affects fertility, focusing on the intergenerational allocation of family-sized homes and the roles of ownership and financial constraints. Using PSID and ACS data, I document that households occupy larger dwellings and are more likely to own after the first birth. Housing-market segmentation and borrowing constraints jointly limit access to family-sized homes: these units are concentrated in the ownership market, where down-payment requirements exclude many young households with little liquid wealth. At the same time, older households own much of the stock of large homes and often remain in them after their children leave.

    I develop a lifecycle model of fertility, housing, saving, and tenure choice with transaction costs and bequests to study how this allocation affects the cost of having children. I calibrate the model to the U.S. economy in 2007 and estimate a shock to preferences to study the transition associated with the subsequent decline in fertility. Housing demand, prices, and the allocation of homes across generations adjust as the population evolves. Along this transition, I compare policies that ease young households’ borrowing constraints with incentives for older owners to release larger homes, assessing their effects on access to family-sized housing and fertility.

  • Consumption Sorting and Inequality (with Saverio Spinella) — Draft (coming soon)
    Abstract

    Households cluster in space by income and consumption. We study how spatial inequality shapes local retail prices. In our model, households invest time comparing prices across stores to spend less. A high concentration of retailers makes it easier to find better deals, while a high income reduces the incentive to search for bargains. Retailers respond to their customers’ shopping behavior: rich neighborhoods attract more shops, especially those selling high-quality goods.

    Using NielsenIQ data, we document that households pay more for the same basket in richer neighborhoods, even conditional on their own income. Yet goods favored by richer households are relatively cheaper in richer locations. Retail markets thus respond to income and consumption segregation and sustain it. We use the calibrated model to compare affordability policies. Production subsidies are more progressive than price caps, but price caps are more effective at reducing segregation.

  • Frictional Rental Markets and Inequality — New draft (coming soon)
    Abstract

    Using ACS microdata, I document new facts on the heterogeneity of paid rents across individuals and cities. First, young people tend to pay higher rents and move more frequently, especially in denser and less housing-elastic cities. Second, recent movers—both within and across cities—pay systematically higher rents. These effects are heterogeneous across space and stronger in larger, denser metropolitan areas.

    To rationalize these patterns, I develop a parsimonious model of directed search in the rental market. The model explains the observed dispersion in paid rents and implies that increases in housing supply would disproportionately benefit younger and more mobile households.
